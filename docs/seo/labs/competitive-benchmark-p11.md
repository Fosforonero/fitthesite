# Competitive Benchmark Synthesis: HRV & Sleep Efficiency Calculators for FitMesh Labs

Eight sources were reviewed: SleepFoundation.org (sleep efficiency, prose-only), the DiMe Society / DATAcc digital-measure spec + its underlying JCSM paper (Reed & Sacco 2016), Kubios HRV (Scientific + consumer App), Elite HRV, WHOOP's "The Locker," Oura Ring's public HRV/Sleep Score content, Omni Calculator's Sleep Calculator (no HRV calculator exists on the site), and Fitbit/Google Health + Garmin (Firstbeat Analytics) Sleep Score documentation. ## 2. Common UX Patterns Across Simple/Authoritative Calculators

- **Worked numeric example alongside the formula** is the most common trust device (SleepFoundation, WHOOP, DiMe's JCSM source) — but the example is always generic/textbook, never the reader's own numbers.
- **Categorical, color-coded bands** dominate results presentation (Oura's Optimal/Good/Fair/Pay Attention, Elite HRV's traffic light, Fitbit's Excellent/Good/Fair/Poor, Garmin's poor→excellent). Nearly every "score" product converges on 4–5 tiers.
- **"Compare to your own trend, not to others"** is a repeated, near-universal framing (WHOOP, Oura, Elite HRV all say this explicitly) — population comparisons exist but are hedged.
- **Two-tier depth without a real in-page toggle**: simple/plain-language lives on the main product or marketing page; "advanced" content is a *separate* page/PDF/KB article a user must click away to reach (Kubios Scientific vs. App, Elite HRV marketing site vs. help.elitehrv.com, DiMe page 1 vs. page 2, Garmin's opt-in overlays being the closest thing to an actual in-page toggle).
- **Named human/authorial bylines** are used as a credibility signal independent of citations (SleepFoundation's MD reviewer, Omni's named PhD creator + COO reviewer, WHOOP's named data-science lead byline).
- **Disclaimers exist but are structurally distant from the result** — generic site-wide boilerplate (SleepFoundation), buried in a bottom FAQ (WHOOP), on a separate differently-titled article (Oura), or scattered across community pages (Fitbit/Garmin). Nobody puts the disclaimer *next to the number*.
- **Proprietary composite scores hide the weighting even when inputs are named** — Oura names 7 "contributors" with target ranges but never the combination math; Fitbit named 6 metrics but no arithmetic; Kubios's readiness index and Elite HRV's 0–100 score are calibrated against undisclosed internal databases.
- **No source in the set shows the formula run against the user's own measured data on the same screen.** This is the single clearest, universal gap.

## 3. Common Accessibility Gaps (FitMesh's Differentiation Opportunity)

Across all eight sources, accessibility was either unconfirmable (research method limits noted honestly in the raw data) or, where confirmable, actively deficient:

- **No accessibility statement or WCAG-conformance claim was found anywhere** — not on Kubios, Elite HRV, WHOOP, Oura, Omni, Fitbit, or Garmin's public pages.
- **Chart/diagram-only data with no text or table equivalent**: WHOOP's charts carry topic-level alt text ("heart rate variability chart ms") but no data-level values; the DiMe timeline diagram (rest period/onset latency/primary sleep period with lightning-bolt wake icons) has no adjacent text-table equivalent, meaning its meaning would likely be lost to a screen reader.
- **Heading-hierarchy violations**: WHOOP marks chart captions as H6 directly under H2, skipping H3–H5 — a confirmed WCAG best-practice violation.
- **Math rendered as non-text images**: Kubios's formulas on its methods page appear to be LaTeX/math images with unconfirmed alt text or MathML — a known accessibility risk pattern.
- **Color-coded severity tiers with no confirmed redundant (non-color) encoding**: Fitbit/Garmin's poor→excellent bands and Elite HRV's traffic light read as implicit red→green ramps in screenshots, with no confirmed icon/pattern/text redundancy.
- **Client-side JS shells block fetch/crawler/some-AT access entirely**: WHOOP's support.whoop.com (Salesforce Lightning SPA) returns an empty shell without full JS execution; Garmin's official FAQ page 404s to non-browser fetches.
- **Positive counter-examples worth matching, not just beating**: WHOOP's Locker pages do have correct landmark roles, one real H1 per article, and descriptive (if topic-level) alt text; Omni's Sleep Calculator has real semantic heading hierarchy and true list markup (not divs) plus alt text on images. These represent the accessibility *floor* competitors already clear — FitMesh needs to clear WCAG 2.2 AA properly, not just match this floor.

**The opportunity**: not one of the eight sources can currently claim WCAG 2.2 AA conformance with confidence, and the two calculator-like tools in the set (SleepFoundation's static prose, Omni's manual Sleep Calculator) are exactly the format FitMesh Labs is building. Shipping data-table alternatives to every chart, a real accessibility statement, redundant (color+icon+text) band encoding, proper heading hierarchy, and accessible math markup would be a genuine, verifiable, citable differentiator — not just a marketing claim.

## 4. Concrete Design Decisions for FitMesh Labs

### 4a. HRV RMSSD Calculator (retrofit)

**Simple mode (default view):**
- User enters/imports RR intervals (manual paste of a short list, or "sync from your FitMesh ring" for logged-in users — the funnel hook Omni structurally can't offer).
- Output: RMSSD in ms + FitMesh's own plain-language band (avoid inventing a new opaque 0–100 "score" like Elite HRV/Kubios — either show raw ms with an age/sex-adjusted normative range, or if a composite score is used, publish its weights, see 4c).
- One-line, always-visible disclaimer directly under the result: e.g. "Misura di benessere, non diagnosi medica" — co-located, not buried in a bottom FAQ (the WHOOP failure) or a separate article (the Oura failure).

**Advanced mode (expand/tap, not a separate page):**
- Render the actual RMSSD formula in KaTeX:
  `RMSSD = \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(RR_{i+1}-RR_i)^2}`
- Immediately below the KaTeX block, substitute the **user's own RR-interval sequence** into the same formula step by step (successive differences → squares → mean → square root) — this is the one thing none of the eight competitors do (Kubios shows math only on a detached blog page never tied to the reader's data; Elite HRV never shows math at all; WHOOP names RMSSD but never renders it).
- Add a KaTeX accessibility fallback: pair every rendered formula with a plain-text `aria-label` / adjacent `<span class="sr-only">` spelling out the equation in words, so screen-reader users get the same content non-visually (closing the Kubios-style image/LaTeX-with-unknown-alt-text gap).

**Source citation strategy:**
- Hyperlinked, DOI-resolving inline citations (not "backed by research" vague claims like Elite HRV, not detached blog-only citations like WHOOP): Shaffer & Ginsberg 2017 (*Frontiers in Public Health*, HRV norms), the 1996 Task Force of the ESC/NASPE HRV standards paper.
- If any percentile/normative comparison is shown, disclose sample size and any known skew inline on the same screen (Elite HRV's male/athlete-skewed norm data was disclosed only in a separate blog post — never repeat that pattern).

**Results visualization:**
- A small tachogram/scatter of the user's actual successive RR-interval differences (not a generic stock diagram like Omni's borrowed hypnogram image), with a data-table toggle showing the same values in text form for accessibility.
- If a continuous-monitoring trend is available (ring vs. Elite HRV's single 1–5 min session or Kubios's fasted single reading), lead with that as the real differentiator: passive/continuous vs. competitors' mandatory active sit-still protocol.

### 4b. NEW Sleep Efficiency Calculator

**Simple mode:**
- Inputs: time in bed / time asleep (manual entry) or auto-populated from ring sleep-stage data for logged-in users.
- Output: percentage + FitMesh's consistent band taxonomy (reuse across both calculators — don't invent a second incompatible scale), with an inline, tappable one-line disclosure of exactly which denominator was used: *"calculated from time in bed, per DiMe Society digital sleep-measurement guidance."* This directly resolves the ambiguity Reed & Sacco (2016) raised, that SleepFoundation cites but never explains, and that DiMe's own spec explicitly requires implementers to disclose.

**Advanced mode:**
- KaTeX formula, shown as DiMe presents it (denominator explicitly labeled, not silently assumed):
  `SE = \dfrac{TST}{TIB}\times 100\%` with an inline footnote-style toggle to the DiMe-preferred `SE = \dfrac{TST}{T_{attempt}}\times 100\%` where "time attempting to sleep" data exists.
- A timeline visualization using the **user's actual night**, not a generic borrowed diagram: lie-down → sleep-onset latency → primary sleep period broken into real detected awake/light/deep/REM segments → wake. Pair this with a text/table alternative listing the same segments and durations (closing the exact accessibility gap in both DiMe's own PDF diagram and WHOOP's chart-only breakdowns).
- Optional: surface the Sleep Restriction Therapy 3-tier rule (SleepFoundation describes this only as a manual paper-diary protocol) as an automated, opt-in coaching nudge — a rolling 7–14 day efficiency average driving an actual bedtime recommendation, turning static prose into a working feature no pure content site can offer.

**Source citation strategy:**
- Cite Reed & Sacco (2016, *JCSM*) directly and **explain the nuance** rather than citing it only to support a flat threshold (SleepFoundation's specific failure) — one sentence: "efficiency depends on how 'time in bed' is defined; we use X, other tools may use Y, which is why numbers aren't always comparable across apps."
- Cite the DiMe/DATAcc spec itself as the methodology standard FitMesh follows, since disclosing denominator methodology is something no consumer sleep-tracking competitor in this set currently does.

### 4c. Shared design system across both calculators
- One consistent band taxonomy and disclaimer placement pattern used everywhere (not Oura's drift between blog and support-center near-duplicate content).
- If any composite/weighted score is introduced later, publish the literal weights on the same screen as the result — e.g., "Score 68 = 34 pts duration + 20 pts quality + 14 pts restoration" — since FitMesh's insight engine is already deterministic/rule-based (non-AI), this is a truthful claim none of Oura/Fitbit/Garmin/Kubios/Elite HRV can honestly make.
- WCAG 2.2 AA specifics to bake in from day one: proper heading hierarchy (no skipped levels, unlike WHOOP), redundant color+icon+text encoding for every band/tier, real semantic list/table markup (not divs), visible focus states and full keyboard operability on all inputs/toggles, an actual published accessibility statement (something zero competitors in this set have), and accessible-text fallbacks for every KaTeX-rendered formula.

## 5. Explicit List: What NOT to Copy

- **SleepFoundation**: Do not fragment one concept across multiple uncrossed-linked pages with no canonical URL. Do not cite a paper about denominator ambiguity (Reed & Sacco 2016) and then ignore the ambiguity it raises.
- **DiMe/DATAcc**: Do not ship the denominator-disclosure diagram as a pure visual with no text/table equivalent — the mandate to disclose methodology is worth keeping, the inaccessible diagram is not.
- **Kubios**: Do not split transparency all-or-nothing (real math only for researchers on a separate page, zero math for the consumer-facing score). Do not omit a non-medical-device disclaimer from the product pages themselves.
- **Elite HRV**: Do not build a proprietary black-box scaling function with no published formula. Do not let credibility claims (sample sizes, "backed by hundreds of papers") go unverified or drift inconsistently across your own pages. Do not disclose sample bias/skew only in a detached blog post instead of on the comparison screen itself.
- **WHOOP**: Do not bury the one real disclaimer at the bottom of a long article. Do not bookend every educational surface with subscription CTAs in a way that tilts framing toward marketing over neutral health communication. Do not build any geo-IP redirect logic that can silently 404 a URL.
- **Oura**: Do not name contributing factors/target ranges while withholding the actual weighting math — that's a "transparent inputs, opaque math" pattern, not real transparency. Do not maintain near-duplicate content across two properties (blog vs. support) that can drift out of sync over time.
- **Omni Calculator**: Do not ship a "calculator" that never shows its own formula. Do not illustrate results with a generic, non-personalized stock image instead of the user's actual data. Do not add fear-driven statistical engagement bait (e.g., a bold mortality-risk percentage) adjacent to a wellness tool without heavy, inline caveats.
- **Fitbit/Google Health**: Do not gate the diagnostic "why did my score change" breakdown behind a paywall — that punishes exactly the free users who most need the explanation. Do not regress transparency during a "redesign" (Fitbit's 2026 redesign dropped even its old rough percentage weights).
- **Garmin**: Do not let genuine peer-reviewed validation science (the Firstbeat white paper, the JMIR PSG study) exist without linking it from any page a user would actually land on — real evidence that's undiscoverable provides no trust benefit to the reader.
- **All eight, generally**: Do not ship a "calculator" that never actually runs the formula against the user's own numbers on-screen — every source in this set fails this test in some way, and it is the single biggest opportunity for FitMesh Labs to differentiate. Do not skip an accessibility statement or ship charts/diagrams without a text/table equivalent — no competitor in this set can currently claim WCAG 2.2 AA, so doing so credibly is a real, defensible, and citable differentiator, not busywork.

Disclaimers are generic site-wide boilerplate ("does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment"; "a doctor is in the best position to make a detailed recommendation") rather than anything specific to the efficiency metric's limitations.

**Fonti citate da loro:** Citations follow a site-wide "Trusted Source" inline hover-link convention pointing to PubMed/NCBI, plus a numbered reference list at each article's end (hyperlinked to PubMed/DOI where available). The recurring, load-bearing citation for the "85% is healthy" claim across multiple articles is: Reed, D.L. & Sacco, W.P. (2016), "Measuring sleep efficiency: What should the denominator be?", Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 12(2), 263-266 — notably a paper whose actual point is that the denominator (what counts as "time in bed") is methodologically contested, a nuance SleepFoundation never passes on to readers even though it cites the paper repeatedly. Some articles (e.g., "How Is Sleep Quality Calculated?") carry an MD medical-reviewer byline (Anis Rehman, MD); the glossary/dictionary entry, by contrast, cites no sources at all.


**Punti deboli:** (1) No single canonical URL for "sleep efficiency" — a reader searching the concept lands on six different articles with slightly different examples and framing, none cross-linked as a definitive source. (3) The load-bearing "85%" citation (Reed & Sacco 2016) is a paper about denominator ambiguity in efficiency measurement, yet SleepFoundation uses it only to support a flat threshold, never surfacing that the metric's definition itself is contested in the literature they cite. (4) No treatment of how a consumer wearable/ring would actually derive "time asleep" (actigraphy vs. PPG-based staging vs. manual diary) — a live gap given the whole site talks about sleep tracking devices elsewhere (they review Oura Ring, etc.) but never connects device-derived sleep efficiency methodology to the definition pages. (5) No visual representation of the metric anywhere, despite it being an inherently visual proportion.


**Opportunità per FitMesh:** SleepFoundation cites the one paper that says the denominator for "time in bed" is ambiguous, then quietly ignores that ambiguity and gives readers a static 85% rule with no way to see their own numbers. FitMesh Labs, with actual ring-derived sleep-stage data, could differentiate by (a) showing the sleep efficiency formula live against the user's own night — a simple time-in-bed vs. time-asleep bar broken into the actual detected segments (awake/light/deep/REM), not just a bare percentage — something no page on SleepFoundation does for any reader; and (b) being explicit and transparent about exactly what counts as the numerator and denominator for a ring (e.g., "time in bed" = from lie-down detection to get-up detection; "asleep" = ring's staging classifier output), directly resolving the ambiguity Reed & Sacco (2016) raised and SleepFoundation cites but never explains. A second, more actionable angle: SleepFoundation describes the CBT-I sleep-restriction 85/90% three-tier bedtime-adjustment rule only as something a person does manually with a paper diary — FitMesh could automate that exact clinically-grounded protocol (rolling 1-2 week efficiency average → automatic bedtime nudge/recommendation), turning a static prose explanation into a functioning in-app coaching feature that SleepFoundation, as a pure content site, structurally cannot offer.


### 2. DiMe Society / DATAcc — "Core Digital Measure of Sleep: Sleep Efficiency (Percentage)" specification sheet (2-page PDF, part of the "Core Measures of Sleep" digital-measures library), which in turn is

**URL/fonti:** Primary fetched doc: https://dimesociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Core-Digital-Measure-of-Sleep-Sleep-efficiency-Percentage.pdf . Cited peer-reviewed source (AASM's journal, JCSM): publisher page https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.5498 (direct fetch failed with an SSL cert error on this domain) — full text instead retrieved successfully via the NIH mirror https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4751425/ . Abstract cross-checked at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26194727/ .


**Input:** The spec decomposes sleep efficiency into raw/derived inputs rather than asking a user to type numbers into a calculator (it's a measure-development standard, not a consumer tool). Required concepts: (1) Total Sleep Time (TST) — sum of epochs labeled "asleep" within the primary sleep period; (2) "Time attempting to sleep" start time and end time — timestamps marking when the individual intends to start/stop sleeping (can come from user self-report or be inferred by an algorithm); (3) "Time attempting to sleep duration" — a derived variable (seconds) = end time minus start time, which becomes the formula's denominator. The doc explicitly names "Optional deviations": where true intention-to-sleep data isn't captured (the norm for most consumer wearables, since intention is "non-observable"), it permits substituting the classic Time-In-Bed (TIB) or the "primary sleep period" as a proxy denominator — but mandates that this substitution be disclosed in the product's own measure specification. The underlying JCSM paper (Reed & Sacco 2016) frames the same inputs as SOL (sleep onset latency), TST, WASO (wake after sleep onset), and TASAFA (time attempting to sleep after final awakening), which sum to their proposed "Duration of Sleep Episode" (DSE) denominator.


**Risultati mostrati:** Sleep efficiency is a single output type: a percentage (0-100%), labeled "Outcome → Percentage" in the spec's concept map. No example computed value or reference range (e.g., "normal ≥85%") is given in the DiMe document itself. The JCSM source paper, by contrast, does walk through one worked numeric example to make the denominator problem concrete: TST 300 min, TIB 545 min → SE 55%; same TST 300 min against DSE 470 min → SE 64% — a 9-point swing from denominator choice alone, presented as a table/example, not just an abstract formula.


**Presentazione formula:** Shown twice, consistently: as a labeled equation directly under the timeline diagram ("Sleep efficiency = Total sleep time / Time attempting to sleep duration × 100") and again in prose on page 2 under "Measure derivation" ("The total sleep time divided by the time attempting to sleep duration. Multiplying this value by 100 expresses sleep efficiency as a percentage"). The formula's denominator term is deliberately not called "time in bed" but "time attempting to sleep duration," with a footnoted caveat that TIB is only an acceptable substitute when the preferred input isn't collected — the formula is explicitly presented as one instance of an unsettled methodological choice, not a fixed constant, and is directly footnoted to the Reed & Sacco (2016) JCSM reference (reference #1 on page 2).


**Note UX:** This isn't a consumer-facing tool, so "simple vs advanced mode" doesn't apply literally, but the document is laid out in an analogous two-tier way: page 1 gives a plain-language definition ("the proportion of time spent sleeping when the individual intends to sleep") plus a horizontal timeline diagram (In bed → rest period → initial sleep-onset latency → primary sleep period with "Asleep" segments and lightning-bolt "wake event" icons → sleep-offset latency → rest period → Out of bed, with bracket annotations for "Time in bed" and "Time attempting to sleep") followed immediately by the formula — definition before symbol, diagram before math. Page 2 is the "advanced" layer: precise derivation rules, edge-case handling ("optional deviations"), and the citation. The one clear disclosure pattern worth noting: the spec doesn't just define the ideal formula, it explicitly requires implementers to state which denominator convention they actually used — a built-in transparency requirement baked into the standard itself, which is unusual and notable.


**Note accessibilità:** No accessibility features or considerations are mentioned anywhere in the DiMe document. The core explanatory artifact (the timeline diagram with nested "rest period / primary sleep period" spans, a teal highlight box, dashed connector lines, and lightning-bolt wake-event icons) is purely visual/spatial with no adjacent text-table equivalent conveying the same relationships — meaning the diagram's meaning would likely be lost to a screen-reader user reading the raw PDF text linearly, and I found no evidence (nor did the doc state) that the PDF is tagged for accessibility. This is a gap worth avoiding in FitMesh's own in-app version of this diagram: pair any such timeline visualization with a text/list alternative and proper semantic labeling.


**Fonti citate da loro:** The DiMe/DATAcc spec cites exactly one reference: Reed, D.L., & Sacco, W.P. (2016). "Measuring sleep efficiency: what should the denominator be?" Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 12(2), 263-266 — published in JCSM, the AASM's own official peer-reviewed journal. I was able to retrieve that paper's full text via its NIH/PMC mirror (PMC4751425) after a direct fetch of the jcsm.aasm.org page failed with a certificate-verification error; within the portion of that paper I could access, I did not see it cite the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events by name (it engages with the SE literature/denominator debate generally) — I'm flagging this explicitly as something I could not fully confirm rather than guessing, since I never got clean direct access to the AASM-hosted page itself.


**Punti deboli:** (1) No universal denominator: the AASM's own journal published a paper saying the field is still actively divided on whether SE's denominator should be TIB, a "sleep opportunity window," or a diary-derived "time attempting to sleep" — meaning SE percentages are not directly comparable across devices/apps unless the convention is disclosed. (2) The classic TIB formula penalizes non-sleep in-bed behavior (reading, texting) that has nothing to do with sleep quality, which is exactly the validity critique the cited paper makes. (3) "Intention to sleep" — needed for the more defensible DSE-style denominator — is explicitly called "non-observable" by the spec itself, meaning most consumer wearables (accelerometer/PPG rings included) structurally cannot compute the "better" version and must silently fall back to a proxy. (4) Zero consumer-facing content: no plain explanation of what a given percentage means for the user, no severity bands, no example walkthrough in the DiMe doc (only in the academic paper).
